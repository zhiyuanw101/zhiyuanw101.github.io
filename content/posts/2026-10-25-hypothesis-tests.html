Data are noisy. An observed effect, such as a coin that lands heads more often than tails, can be real or the result of chance. A hypothesis test is a fixed procedure that decides whether chance is a sufficient explanation.

This post gives the procedure, the p-value and the two types of error. Three worked examples follow: a binomial test, a one-sample $t$-test and the partial $F$ test from Linear Regression Models.

The Procedure

Every test has the same five parts:

  1. Null hypothesis $H_0$: the default claim, usually “no effect”. It must be precise, so that probabilities under it can be calculated.
  2. Alternative hypothesis $H_a$: the claim to accept if the data reject $H_0$.
  3. Test statistic $T$: a number calculated from the data that measures the evidence against $H_0$.
  4. Null distribution: the distribution of $T$ when $H_0$ is true. It shows which values chance alone produces.
  5. Decision rule: before looking at the data, choose a significance level $\alpha$, usually 0.05. Reject $H_0$ if the p-value is less than $\alpha$.

The data are random, so $T$ is random. Calculated from one data set, $T$ is one number. Across repeated studies, $T$ varies, and the null distribution describes that variation under $H_0$.

The p-value

p-value

The probability, if $H_0$ is true, of a test statistic at least as extreme as the observed value $t_{\text{obs}}$. When large values of $T$ are evidence against $H_0$:

$$ p = P\big(T \ge t_{\text{obs}} \mid H_0\big) $$

Thus the p-value is the area of the null distribution beyond the observed value (Fig. 1). A small p-value means that chance rarely produces a result like the observed one. A large p-value means that chance produces such results often.

Decision rule at level $\alpha$

Reject $H_0$ if $p < \alpha$. Equivalently, reject $H_0$ if $t_{\text{obs}}$ is beyond the critical value, the point where the null distribution has tail area $\alpha$.

Whether “extreme” means large, small or far from the center in both directions depends on $H_a$ ().

A one-sided alternative states a direction, for example “the coin favors heads”. Only one tail of the null distribution counts as extreme. A two-sided alternative, “the coin is not fair”, counts both tails.

For 60 heads in 100 flips, the one-sided p-value is $P(X \ge 60) = 0.028$. The two-sided p-value is $P(|X - 50| \ge 10) = 0.057$. At $\alpha = 0.05$, the one-sided test rejects $H_0$, but the two-sided test does not. Choose the alternative before you look at the data; a choice made after the data invalidates the p-value.

What a p-value is not

  • It is not the probability that $H_0$ is true. $p = 0.03$ does not mean a 3% chance of no effect.
  • It is not the size or the importance of an effect. With a large sample, a negligible effect can give a small p-value.
  • A large p-value does not prove $H_0$. It means that the data give no evidence against $H_0$; the sample can be too small to detect a real effect.

Two Types of Error

$H_0$ is true$H_0$ is false
Reject $H_0$Type I error (probability $\alpha$)correct decision (probability = power)
Do not reject $H_0$correct decisionType II error

The level $\alpha$ is the probability of a Type I error: if $H_0$ is true, a test at $\alpha = 0.05$ rejects it in 5% of studies. The power is the probability of rejecting a false $H_0$. Power increases with the sample size and with the size of the real effect.

Examples

Example 1: is a coin fair?

A coin gives 60 heads in 100 flips. Does it favor heads?

$H_0$the coin is fair, $P(\text{heads}) = 0.5$
$H_a$the coin favors heads, $P(\text{heads}) > 0.5$
Test statisticnumber of heads, $X = 60$
Null distribution$X \sim \text{Binomial}(100, 0.5)$: mean 50, standard deviation 5
p-value$P(X \ge 60) = 0.028$ (Fig. 1)
Decision$0.028 < 0.05$: reject $H_0$. The coin favors heads.

With 53 heads, $p = P(X \ge 53) = 0.31$. A fair coin gives such a result in about one of three studies, so the test does not reject $H_0$.

Example 2: does a machine underfill bags?

A machine fills bags labeled 500 g. A sample of $n = 16$ bags has mean $\bar{x} = 497.2$ g and standard deviation $s = 4.8$ g. Assume that the fill weights are normal.

$H_0$the mean fill weight is $\mu = 500$ g
$H_a$the machine underfills, $\mu < 500$ g
Test statistic$t = \dfrac{\bar{x} - 500}{s/\sqrt{n}} = \dfrac{497.2 - 500}{4.8/4} = -2.33$
Null distribution$t \sim t_{15}$, the $t$ distribution with $n - 1 = 15$ degrees of freedom
p-value$P(t_{15} \le -2.33) = 0.017$; the critical value is $-1.753$
Decision$0.017 < 0.05$: reject $H_0$. The machine underfills.

The statistic divides the difference from 500 g by its estimated standard error. Thus $t$ measures the difference in units of noise, as the $F$ statistic does in Example 3. A confidence interval gives the same decision and also the size of the effect ().

The 95% confidence interval for $\mu$ is $\bar{x} \pm t_{15,\,0.975}\, s/\sqrt{n} = 497.2 \pm 2.131 \times 1.2 = [494.6,\ 499.8]$ g. It contains exactly the values $\mu_0$ that a two-sided test at $\alpha = 0.05$ does not reject.

The value 500 g is outside the interval, and the two-sided p-value is $0.034 < 0.05$. The interval also shows the size of the effect: the machine underfills by between 0.2 g and 5.4 g.

Example 3: do the father’s characteristics matter?

In the birthweight study of Linear Regression Models, a reduced model uses 8 predictors and the full model adds the father’s 4 predictors. There are $n = 42$ babies.

$H_0$the father’s 4 coefficients are 0
$H_a$at least one of the 4 coefficients is not 0
Test statistic$F = \dfrac{(\text{RSS}_0 - \text{RSS}_a)/4}{\text{RSS}_a/29} = 0.7225$
Null distribution$F \sim F_{4,\,29}$
p-value$P(F_{4,29} \ge 0.7225) = 0.58$; the critical value is 2.70
Decision$0.58 > 0.05$: do not reject $H_0$. The data give no evidence that the father’s characteristics help.

$F$ is the decrease in RSS per added predictor, divided by the noise level $\hat\sigma^2$. If the added predictors are useless, $F$ is near 1. Here $F = 0.72$, a common value under $H_0$.

Quick Summary

  • A test assumes the precise null hypothesis $H_0$, calculates a test statistic from the data and compares it with the null distribution.
  • The p-value is the probability, under $H_0$, of a result at least as extreme as the observed one. Reject $H_0$ if $p < \alpha$.
  • A p-value is not the probability that $H_0$ is true, and a large p-value does not prove $H_0$.
  • $\alpha$ is the probability of a Type I error; power is the probability of rejecting a false $H_0$.
  • Only the statistic and its null distribution change between tests: binomial for counts, $t$ for one mean or coefficient, $F$ for nested regression models.
